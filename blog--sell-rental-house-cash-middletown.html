What Does a Cash Offer Actually Look Like for a Middletown Rental?

Cash buyers use a transparent formula to calculate their offers. For more details, see sell your rental as-is in Middletown. Understanding this formula helps you evaluate whether a cash sale makes sense for your situation.

The Formula:

Maximum Allowable Offer = (After-Repair Value × 70%) − Estimated Repairs

Here's a real example for a Middletown rental:

  • Property address: 1234 Main Street, Middletown, OH
  • After-Repair Value (ARV): $160,000 (what the property would be worth fully renovated and tenant-ready)
  • Estimated repairs: $25,000 (roof, HVAC, flooring, paint)
  • Buyer's calculation: ($160,000 × 70%) − $25,000 = $112,000 − $25,000 = $87,000 cash offer

The 70% figure accounts for the buyer's holding costs (property taxes, insurance, utilities while vacant), closing costs, and profit margin. It's not arbitrary - it's the industry standard documented across real estate investor education platforms.

If you listed that same $160,000 property with an agent:

  • Agent commission (5–6%): −$9,600
  • Repairs before listing: −$25,000
  • Holding costs (75 days on market): −$2,500
  • Net proceeds: ~$123,000

Can You Sell a Tenant-Occupied Rental for Cash in Middletown?

Yes, absolutely. This is one of the biggest advantages of a cash sale and a major gap in traditional listings. Read more about how cash buyers handle tenant-occupied rentals in Middletown.

Under Ohio Revised Code § 5321.18, when a property is sold, the new owner steps into the landlord's shoes and assumes any existing lease. A fixed-term lease continues in full force until its expiration date - the buyer cannot void it simply by purchasing the property. This is the law, and it applies to all sales, cash or financed.

Here's how three common tenant scenarios play out:

Scenario 1: Fixed-Term Lease (e.g., 12-month lease with 8 months remaining)

The lease transfers to the new owner at closing. The buyer becomes the landlord and collects rent for the remaining 8 months. You're done. No eviction needed. No tenant drama. The buyer knew about the lease when they made the offer, so it's already factored into their price.

Scenario 2: Month-to-Month Tenant

The lease transfers, but the new owner can terminate it with 30 days' written notice under ORC § 5321.17. If the buyer wants vacant possession, they serve notice after closing. You don't have to evict anyone - the buyer handles it. Your sale closes while the tenant is still there.

Scenario 3: Non-Paying or Problem Tenant

This is where cash buyers shine. A buyer experienced with rentals knows how to handle a tenant in default or causing damage. They'll either work with the tenant, serve notice, or handle eviction if necessary. You don't have to manage that conflict. The buyer assumes the liability and the tenant relationship at closing.

Why traditional buyers avoid tenant-occupied rentals:

Conventional buyers often require vacant possession or demand a rent credit. This forces you to evict the tenant before closing - a costly, time-consuming process in Ohio that can take 30–60 days. Cash buyers, by contrast, are accustomed to tenant-occupied properties and price them accordingly. No eviction required.

Tax Considerations When Selling a Rental Property in Ohio

Selling a rental property triggers two tax layers: federal capital gains tax and Ohio state income tax. Understanding these costs helps you plan your exit strategy and avoid surprises at tax time.

Capital Gains Tax (Federal)

If you've owned the rental for more than one year, your gain qualifies for long-term capital gains rates: 0%, 15%, or 20% federally, depending on your total taxable income. The gain is calculated as the sale price minus your original purchase price and any capital improvements you've made.

Example: You bought the rental for $100,000 and sell it for $160,000. Your gain is $60,000. If you're in the 15% long-term capital gains bracket, you owe $9,000 in federal tax on that gain.

Depreciation Recapture (Federal)

Here's the catch that surprises many landlords: depreciation recapture is taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25%, regardless of your income bracket. This applies to all the depreciation you've claimed on your tax returns during your ownership period.

If you claimed $30,000 in depreciation over 10 years, you owe 25% of that ($7,500) in depreciation recapture tax when you sell - in addition to capital gains tax on your profit. This applies to cash sales and financed sales equally; the sale method doesn't eliminate the tax.

Ohio State Income Tax

Ohio taxes capital gains as ordinary income at your state marginal rate, which topped out at 3.99% in 2024. So if your federal long-term capital gains rate is 15%, add Ohio's 3.99% on top for a combined 18.99% rate on your gain.

1031 Exchange as an Alternative

If you want to defer capital gains and depreciation recapture taxes, a 1031 exchange allows you to reinvest your sale proceeds into another qualifying investment property within 180 days (with a 45-day identification deadline). The taxes are deferred rather than eliminated - you'll eventually owe them when you sell the replacement property without conducting another exchange. A cash sale is compatible with a 1031 exchange - you simply route the proceeds through a qualified intermediary to preserve the tax-deferred status. The IRS also provides guidance on the mechanics and requirements in their Like-kind exchanges - Real estate tax tips resource, which is worth reviewing before you proceed.

Bottom line: Consult a tax professional before selling. The tax bill can be substantial, but planning ahead (1031 exchange, timing of the sale, depreciation recapture strategy) can minimize what you owe.
